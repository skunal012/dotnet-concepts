Name the five guarantees a method call gives you that a network call does not.	1. <b>It happens.</b><br>2. <b>Exactly once.</b><br>3. <b>Now.</b><br>4. <b>It returns or throws</b> — only two outcomes.<br>5. <b>The compiler checked the signature.</b><br><br>Distributed systems is the bill for losing all five.	dotnet::distributed core leech-risk
What are the two axes that choose a transport?	<b>Who initiates</b> — client asks, or server tells.<br><b>What survives a failure</b> — is the message gone, or held until the receiver returns?<br><br>REST, gRPC, SignalR and queues are four positions on those two axes.	dotnet::distributed core
Which of REST, gRPC, SignalR and a queue survives the receiver being down, and why?	<b>Only the queue.</b> A broker sits between the parties and outlives both.<br><br>The other three require both ends alive <i>at the same moment</i> — the only copy is in transit, so it's gone.	dotnet::distributed core leech-risk
REST vs gRPC — they're the same shape. What actually differs?	<b>How the contract is expressed and enforced.</b><br><br>REST: convention + JSON, described after the fact by OpenAPI. gRPC: a <code>.proto</code> <b>compiled into types on both sides</b> — rename a field and the client stops compiling instead of receiving nulls.	dotnet::distributed core
When do you pick gRPC over REST?	<b>gRPC between your own services</b> — both ends yours, typed contract is a real safety gain, binary and multiplexed.<br><br><b>REST at the edge</b> — browsers, third parties, anything you don't control. Browsers can't speak gRPC without gRPC-Web and a translating proxy.	dotnet::distributed why
Why does SignalR exist at all?	Because <b>HTTP cannot push</b> — a server has no way to speak to a client that hasn't asked.<br><br>The alternatives are polling (wasteful and always late) or holding a connection open. SignalR is the second, with transport negotiation handled: WebSockets → SSE → long polling.	dotnet::distributed core
SignalR works, you scale to three instances, users report missing updates and there are no errors. Why?	<b>A connection is state held on one instance.</b> Code on server A publishes to a group, and only clients connected to A receive it.<br><br>Server B never heard of the message. Nothing errors — half your users just don't see the update.	dotnet::distributed deployment leech-risk
What is a SignalR backplane, and what second problem does the managed service solve?	A shared bus (Redis) that every server publishes to and subscribes from, so a message reaches all instances.<br><br>Azure SignalR Service also <b>holds the connections itself</b>, which removes the need for <b>sticky sessions</b> — required otherwise, because negotiate and transport requests must hit the same server.	dotnet::distributed deployment
When should you NOT use SignalR?	When <b>the user wouldn't notice a delay</b>.<br><br>SignalR is a stateful connection you now have to operate — reconnection, backplane, sticky routing, connection limits. Polling a REST endpoint is a smaller system that does the job.	dotnet::distributed why
What does a queue actually buy you?	<b>Decoupling in time.</b> The sender writes and is done; the receiver reads whenever it's running.<br><br>So: the consumer can be down (outages become latency), load is absorbed rather than propagated, and competing consumers split work with each message going to exactly one.	dotnet::distributed core
Why is "exactly-once delivery" not something you can buy?	A broker that delivered a message but never got the acknowledgement <b>cannot tell whether the consumer processed it</b>.<br><br>Its only safe choice is to deliver again. Exactly-once <i>processing</i> is achievable — via idempotency in your handler.	dotnet::distributed core leech-risk
How do you make a message handler idempotent?	<b>Key the work by message ID, record what you've processed, make a repeat a no-op.</b><br><br>Design it in from the first message — retrofitting means reconciling data that has already been double-counted.	dotnet::distributed core
What happens to a message that always throws, and what prevents it?	It is <b>redelivered forever</b> — an infinite loop that looks like a busy consumer.<br><br>A <b>dead-letter queue</b> catches it after N attempts.	dotnet::distributed gotcha
Why can't you atomically write a row and publish a message?	<code>SaveChanges</code> is one transaction (ch. 04 §2), but <b>that transaction cannot include the broker</b>.<br><br>Commit-then-publish loses the message on a crash; publish-then-commit announces something that didn't happen.	dotnet::distributed gotcha leech-risk
What is the outbox pattern?	Write the message to a <b>table, in the same transaction as the data</b>. A separate process reads that table and publishes.<br><br>It converts an impossible atomic operation into an at-least-once one — which idempotency already tells you how to handle.	dotnet::distributed core
Why does a retry need <b>jitter</b> and not just backoff?	Backoff stops <i>you</i> hammering a struggling service. <b>Jitter stops every client retrying in lockstep</b>.<br><br>Without it a brief blip becomes a synchronised thundering herd — the same failure shape as a fleet-wide restart.	dotnet::distributed why leech-risk
What is a circuit breaker actually for?	<b>Not to protect you — to stop hammering something already down</b>, and to fail in milliseconds instead of at your timeout.<br><br>Opens after enough failures, half-opens to test, closes when healthy.	dotnet::distributed why
Why does every remote call need a timeout?	Without one, a <b>hung dependency holds your thread and your request forever</b>.<br><br>That's ch. 05's thread pool starvation arriving from outside your process.	dotnet::distributed core
Across five services, how does the logging question change?	From <b>"what did this service do"</b> to <b>"what happened to <i>this request</i>"</b>.<br><br>No single service's log can answer the second. You need a correlation ID propagated across every hop.	dotnet::distributed core
What does .NET give you for tracing without any work?	<code>System.Diagnostics.Activity</code> implements <b>W3C Trace Context</b>, and <code>HttpClient</code> plus the hosting layer automatically send and read the <code>traceparent</code> header.<br><br>A trace ID exists already — the work is getting it into your logs and across your own boundaries.	dotnet::distributed core
Why does a trace stop dead at a queue?	<b>HTTP carries <code>traceparent</code> for you; a message body does not.</b><br><br>Copy the trace context into message metadata on publish and restore it on consume — the broker is exactly the boundary where you most need the trace.	dotnet::distributed gotcha leech-risk
Why is structured logging a prerequisite for correlation?	You cannot filter a million lines by <code>TraceId</code> if <b>the trace ID was concatenated into a string</b> before it left the process.<br><br>Ch. 07 §8's message templates are what make ch. 09 possible.	dotnet::distributed why
Logs, metrics, traces — what does each answer?	<b>Logs</b> — what happened, in detail, for one thing.<br><b>Metrics</b> — how often and how fast, aggregated. Cheap to keep, useless for a single case.<br><b>Traces</b> — where the time went across services, for one request.	dotnet::distributed core
What are the three good reasons to cross a service boundary?	1. <b>Independent deploy cadence</b> — usually the real one.<br>2. <b>Genuinely different scaling profiles</b> — CPU-bound bursty vs I/O-bound steady.<br>3. <b>Team autonomy at real headcount</b> — decisive at 50 engineers, fiction at 5.	dotnet::distributed why
Why can't you have a distributed transaction, and what replaces it?	You cannot wrap two services' databases in one <code>SaveChanges</code>.<br><br>Multi-step operations become <b>sagas</b> — local transactions each with a <b>compensating action</b>. Compensating is doing real work to undo: you issue a refund, you don't un-charge.	dotnet::distributed core leech-risk
What is a distributed monolith, and what is the tell?	Services that must deploy together, share a database, or chain synchronously to serve one request. Every cost of microservices, none of the benefits.<br><br><b>The tell: if two services write to the same tables, they are one service with a network call in the middle.</b>	dotnet::distributed gotcha leech-risk
Why is versioning "forever" once you have services?	Because services <b>deploy independently, so both versions of a contract are live simultaneously</b>.<br><br>Same expand/contract discipline as ch. 04 §8's schema changes, applied to every message and endpoint.	dotnet::distributed deployment
What is the honest default architecture, and when do you extract?	<b>A modular monolith.</b> Enforce boundaries in code — separate projects, no shared entities, explicit interfaces.<br><br>Extract a service when one of the three good reasons genuinely applies to a specific module. Boundaries you can move beat boundaries you guessed at.	dotnet::distributed why
A call times out. What are the three possibilities, and can you tell them apart?	1. It never arrived.<br>2. It arrived, executed fully, and <b>the response was lost coming back</b>.<br>3. It is executing right now, after you gave up.<br><br><b>You cannot distinguish them.</b> The information does not exist where you are standing.	dotnet::distributed core leech-risk
Why is "a timeout is an unknown outcome, not a failure" the sentence that generates everything else?	Because it explains <b>why retries are unsafe by default</b>, why idempotency keys exist, why at-least-once is the only honest delivery choice, why sagas compensate rather than roll back, and why reconciliation jobs exist.<br><br>Teams that read timeouts as failures write retry logic that quietly duplicates work.	dotnet::distributed why leech-risk
