What is the difference between authentication and authorization, mechanically?	<b>Authentication produces data</b> — a <code>ClaimsPrincipal</code> on <code>HttpContext.User</code>.<br><b>Authorization makes a decision</b> against that data.<br><br>One builds the bag of claims; the other evaluates a requirement over it.	dotnet::auth core
401 vs 403 — what does each one actually tell you?	<b>401</b> — no identity was established, or it isn't accepted. The response is a <i>challenge</i>: prove who you are.<br><b>403</b> — authentication worked perfectly. We know who you are and you may not do this. <b>Re-authenticating will not help.</b>	dotnet::auth core
Why must <code>UseAuthentication</code> come before <code>UseAuthorization</code>?	It's a data dependency. Authorization evaluates the principal that authentication <b>creates</b>.<br><br>Reversed, authorization runs against an anonymous user and every <code>[Authorize]</code> endpoint returns 401 regardless of the token sent.	dotnet::auth core
Why must both auth middlewares run <i>after</i> <code>UseRouting</code>?	Authorization needs to read the selected endpoint's <code>[Authorize]</code> <b>metadata</b>.<br><br>Before routing, no endpoint has been chosen and there is no metadata to read. This is exactly why ch. 01's two-stage routing split exists.	dotnet::auth why leech-risk
What does <code>UseAuthentication</code> do when there are no credentials on the request?	<b>Nothing rejecting.</b> It sets an unauthenticated principal and calls <code>next()</code>.<br><br>The request continues. Nothing rejects until <i>authorization</i> reads endpoint metadata and finds a requirement the principal fails.	dotnet::auth gotcha
What happens to <code>[Authorize]</code> if <code>UseAuthorization()</code> is missing from the pipeline?	<b>Nothing — the endpoint is publicly reachable.</b><br><br>The attribute is metadata, and metadata does nothing on its own. Something has to read it. This is an unauthenticated public endpoint that looks protected in the source.	dotnet::auth gotcha leech-risk
What is a "scheme" in ASP.NET Core auth?	A named handler that knows how to turn part of a request into a <code>ClaimsPrincipal</code> — and how to challenge when there isn't one.<br><br>Cookie, JWT bearer, certificate, OIDC. All produce the same principal; the rest of the framework never knows which ran.	dotnet::auth core
Cookies vs bearer tokens — what is the real trade?	<b>Cookies</b>: sent automatically (hence CSRF, hence antiforgery), but sign-out is <i>real</i> — invalidate server-side.<br><b>Bearer</b>: nothing stored server-side (the appeal <i>and</i> the problem), not sent automatically so no CSRF.	dotnet::auth why
What are the four independent checks when validating a JWT?	<b>Signature</b> — minted by a key holder, unaltered.<br><b>Issuer</b> — <i>which</i> key holder.<br><b>Audience</b> — minted for <i>this</i> API.<br><b>Lifetime</b> — <code>exp</code>/<code>nbf</code>.<br><br>Skipping any one breaks a different thing.	dotnet::auth core leech-risk
What specifically breaks if you set <code>ValidateAudience = false</code>?	A token your users legitimately obtained for a <b>different service</b> is now accepted by yours.<br><br>Every other service sharing your identity provider becomes a way in. Most often disabled while debugging and never re-enabled.	dotnet::auth gotcha leech-risk
Why should you configure which signing algorithms are acceptable rather than trusting the token?	Because <b>a token's header is attacker-controlled input</b>.<br><br>That is the lesson of <code>alg: none</code> and of algorithm confusion (submitting an RS256 public key as an HS256 shared secret). Never let a token choose its own validation rules.	dotnet::auth why
Symmetric (HS256) vs asymmetric (RS256) signing — why prefer asymmetric?	With <b>symmetric</b>, the API holds the same secret used to sign — anything that can validate can also <b>mint</b>.<br><br>With <b>asymmetric</b>, the provider signs privately and you validate with a public key from JWKS, rotated without redeploying you.	dotnet::auth why
Why do JWT expiry tests behave unexpectedly?	The default <code>ClockSkew</code> is <b>five minutes</b>.<br><br>A token can validate for up to five minutes past its <code>exp</code> unless you set it to zero.	dotnet::auth gotcha
What is ASP.NET Core Identity, and what is it <i>not</i>?	<b>Is:</b> a membership store — users/roles in EF Core, PBKDF2 password hashing, lockout, 2FA, confirmation and reset tokens.<br><br><b>Is not:</b> an OIDC provider. It issues no JWTs and has no notion of a client application. That's Duende, OpenIddict, or Entra.	dotnet::auth core
What is the default recommendation on owning user accounts?	<b>Delegate authentication, own authorization.</b><br><br>Let the provider prove identity — you never see a password. Keep <i>what they may do</i> in your domain, because that's business logic and it lives with the data.	dotnet::auth why
What is a role, mechanically?	<b>Just a claim of type <code>role</code>.</b> <code>[Authorize(Roles = "Planner")]</code> is a string comparison against claims on the principal.	dotnet::auth core
When do roles stop being enough?	When you write <code>Roles = "Admin,Supervisor,Planner"</code> — you meant a <b>permission</b> and you're enumerating everyone who has it.<br><br>Roles encode <i>who someone is</i>; permission questions are about <i>what they may do</i>. Add a fourth role and you edit every attribute.	dotnet::auth why
What does a policy buy you over a role?	The attribute states the <b>requirement</b>, and <i>who satisfies it</i> becomes a data question answered wherever claims are issued.<br><br>That's the right seam: <code>[Authorize(Policy = "CanApproveLayout")]</code> doesn't change when the set of people changes.	dotnet::auth why
What can neither roles nor policies express, and what do you use instead?	Anything depending on <b>the specific resource</b> — "may this user approve <i>this</i> layout".<br><br>Resource-based authz: <code>authz.AuthorizeAsync(User, layout, policy)</code> <i>inside</i> the handler, after loading the row. No attribute can run after you have the object.	dotnet::auth core leech-risk
Why is an endpoint with no authorization metadata public by default, and how do you invert it?	Protection is <b>opt-in</b>, so forgetting <code>[Authorize]</code> fails <i>open</i> and silently.<br><br>Set <code>FallbackPolicy = RequireAuthenticatedUser()</code>. Now a forgotten attribute 401s until someone deliberately writes <code>[AllowAnonymous]</code>.	dotnet::auth gotcha leech-risk
<code>DefaultPolicy</code> vs <code>FallbackPolicy</code> — what's the difference?	<b><code>DefaultPolicy</code></b> — what a bare <code>[Authorize]</code> means.<br><b><code>FallbackPolicy</code></b> — what applies when there is <b>no</b> authorization metadata at all.<br><br>Setting the first does nothing for the endpoint somebody forgot.	dotnet::auth gotcha
Why is "protected by CORS" not a real statement?	CORS is a <b>browser</b> restriction on which origins may read a response.<br><br>It does nothing to <code>curl</code>, a mobile app, or a server. That endpoint is closed to one category of client and open to everyone else.	dotnet::auth gotcha
<code>UseHttpsRedirection</code> vs <code>UseHsts</code> — which is the actual control?	<b>HSTS.</b> Redirection fixes the <i>current</i> request after it was already sent in clear text — including its cookie.<br><br>HSTS tells the browser to refuse plaintext for this host next time. Redirection is a courtesy; HSTS is the control.	dotnet::auth why
What should you know before turning on HSTS?	<b>It is hard to undo.</b> A browser caching a one-year <code>max-age</code> refuses http to your domain for a year, and <code>preload</code> ships in the browser binary.<br><br>Start with a short <code>max-age</code> and raise it once every subdomain can serve TLS.	dotnet::auth deployment
Behind a TLS-terminating proxy, name the three auth things that break — and the one cause.	<b>Cause:</b> <code>Request.Scheme</code> is <code>http</code>.<br>1. Infinite redirect loop from <code>UseHttpsRedirection</code>.<br>2. <code>Secure</code> cookies never set.<br>3. OIDC generates an <code>http://</code> <code>redirect_uri</code> and is rejected.<br><br>Fix: <code>UseForwardedHeaders</code>, with trusted proxies configured.	dotnet::auth deployment leech-risk
Why must you configure trusted proxies for <code>UseForwardedHeaders</code>?	<code>X-Forwarded-For</code> and <code>X-Forwarded-Proto</code> are <b>client-supplied headers</b>.<br><br>Accepting them from anywhere lets a caller assert their own scheme and IP address.	dotnet::auth deployment
What do the three cookie flags each defend against?	<b><code>Secure</code></b> — never sent over http.<br><b><code>HttpOnly</code></b> — invisible to JavaScript, so an XSS bug can't exfiltrate the session.<br><b><code>SameSite</code></b> — not sent cross-site; the modern CSRF defence.	dotnet::auth core
Is a JWT encrypted?	<b>No.</b> The payload is base64url — encoding, not encryption.<br><br>Anyone holding the token, or able to see it in a log, devtools, proxy trace or crash report, can read every claim. "Signed" means unaltered, not private.	dotnet::auth gotcha leech-risk
How do you revoke a JWT?	<b>You don't.</b> It is self-contained by design — valid from issue until <code>exp</code>, and no logout can reach it.<br><br>Either make the window short (minutes-long access token + revocable server-side refresh token), or stop using self-contained tokens.	dotnet::auth core leech-risk
Why is shipping with <code>ASPNETCORE_ENVIRONMENT=Development</code> a security hole?	The developer exception page renders <b>stack traces, source snippets and often connection strings</b> to whoever triggered the error.<br><br>Same switch as ch. 03's Development-only scope validation — far worse failure mode.	dotnet::auth deployment
